Section 1

Insurance & Medicaid Coverage for AAC

AAC speech generating devices (SGDs) are classified as Durable Medical Equipment (DME) by Medicare, Medicaid, and most private insurers. This means they are a covered medical benefit, not an optional purchase.

Medicaid Coverage

Medicaid is required to cover AAC devices for children in all 50 states under the federal Early and Periodic Screening, Diagnostic, and Treatment (EPSDT) mandate. Many states also extend coverage to adults. Medicaid is often the most reliable funding pathway for AAC.

Private Insurance

Most private health insurance plans cover SGDs as DME. Coverage varies by plan, but the Affordable Care Act requires coverage of rehabilitative and habilitative devices in many marketplace plans.

What You Need

  • SLP evaluation and report documenting the individual's communication needs, abilities, and why AAC is medically necessary
  • Letter of Medical Necessity (LMN) written by the treating SLP, co-signed by a physician
  • Feature matching assessment showing why the specific device or app was selected
  • Pre-authorization from the insurance company before purchasing the device

Common HCPCS Billing Codes

E2500 — SGD, digitized speech, pre-recorded messages
E2502 — SGD, synthesized speech, requiring message formulation
E2504 — SGD, synthesized speech, multiple methods of access
E2506 — SGD, synthesized speech, with integrated devices
E2508 — Accessory for SGD (mounting system)
E2510 — Accessory for SGD (additional hardware)
E2599 — SGD, not otherwise classified
Tip: Always appeal denials. Initial denials are common and do not mean the device is not covered. Request the specific reason for denial in writing, then submit a formal appeal with additional documentation. Many denials are overturned on appeal. Contact your state's Assistive Technology Act program for appeal support.

Section 2

School District Funding Under IDEA

The Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) Part B requires public school districts to provide assistive technology, including AAC devices, if needed for a child to receive a Free Appropriate Public Education (FAPE).

Key Rights Under IDEA

  • AT consideration is mandatory. The IEP team must consider whether the child needs assistive technology at every IEP meeting. This is a legal requirement, not optional.
  • AT must be written into the IEP. If the team determines AT is needed, it must be documented in the IEP as a supplementary aid, service, or accommodation.
  • Schools cannot refuse AT due to cost. Cost alone is not a legitimate reason to deny a child assistive technology that is needed for FAPE.
  • AT includes home use. If the IEP team determines the child needs the device at home to benefit from education, the school must provide it for home use.

Steps to Get AT Through Your School

  1. Request an AT evaluation in writing. Send a letter to the school requesting a formal assistive technology evaluation. Keep a copy.
  2. The school must respond. The district has a legal obligation to evaluate or provide a written explanation for refusal (which you can challenge).
  3. Attend the IEP meeting. Bring documentation from outside providers (SLP reports, medical records) to support the request.
  4. Ensure AT is documented in the IEP. The device, training, and support services should all be written into the IEP.
  5. Contact the district AT specialist. Most districts have an AT coordinator or team. Request their involvement early.

Funding Sources for School AT

  • IDEA Part B — federal special education funding
  • Title I funds — can be used for AT for eligible students
  • State assistive technology programs — often provide device loans to schools
  • Medicaid reimbursement — schools can bill Medicaid for AT services for eligible students
Know your rights. If the school refuses to evaluate for AT or denies a request, you can file a complaint with your state education agency, request mediation, or request a due process hearing. These are federal protections. Consider contacting a parent advocacy organization or disability rights attorney.
Section 3

Early Intervention (Birth to Age 3)

IDEA Part C covers assistive technology for infants and toddlers (birth to age 3) with developmental delays or disabilities. Every state operates an early intervention (EI) program, though the administering agency varies by state.

Your Rights Under Part C

  • AT evaluation is a right. Families can request an assistive technology evaluation at any time. The EI program must evaluate at no cost to the family.
  • AT is included in the IFSP. If the team determines AT is needed, it must be written into the child's Individualized Family Service Plan (IFSP).
  • AAC is never "too early." Research consistently shows that AAC does not inhibit speech development. Early AAC intervention supports language development and reduces frustration behaviors.
  • No cost to families for evaluation. IDEA Part C guarantees that evaluation and assessment are provided at no cost, regardless of family income.

How to Access Your State's EI Program

  • Contact your pediatrician for a referral
  • Call your state's EI program directly (every state has one)
  • Visit the CDC's Early Intervention Contact Directory to find your state's program
  • Visit ECTA Center's Part C Coordinator List
Tip: Start AAC early. Research by Romski & Sevcik (2005) and others demonstrates that introducing AAC to young children supports, rather than hinders, natural speech development. You do not need to "wait and see" before starting AAC.
Section 4

State Assistive Technology Act Programs

Every U.S. state and territory has an Assistive Technology Act program, funded under the federal AT Act. These programs offer a range of free and low-cost services that can help families access AAC devices.

Services Typically Available

  • Device demonstrations — try out AAC devices before committing to a purchase or funding request
  • Short-term device loans — borrow an AAC device for a trial period (usually 2–6 weeks), often at no cost
  • Device reutilization programs — refurbished devices available at low or no cost
  • Funding information & assistance — help navigating insurance, Medicaid, and other funding sources
  • Training — workshops and individual training on AAC devices and software

Find Your State's AT Program

The Association of Assistive Technology Act Programs (ATAP) maintains a directory of every state program:

ATAP State AT Program Directory

These programs are free. AT Act programs are federally funded public services. You do not need a referral, diagnosis, or insurance to access device demonstrations and loans.
Section 5

Vocational Rehabilitation (Adults)

For adults who need AAC to participate in employment, state Vocational Rehabilitation (VR) agencies can fund communication devices and services.

How VR Funding Works

  • VR agencies exist in every state, funded under the Rehabilitation Act (Section 504)
  • Individuals must have a disability that creates a barrier to employment
  • AAC devices can be funded if they are needed to prepare for, secure, retain, or advance in employment
  • VR also covers training on the device and related support services
  • Services are provided based on an Individualized Plan for Employment (IPE)

How to Apply

  1. Contact your state's VR agency (search "[your state] vocational rehabilitation")
  2. Apply for services and attend an eligibility determination meeting
  3. If eligible, work with your VR counselor to include AAC in your IPE
  4. VR may coordinate with your insurance or Medicaid to maximize coverage

Find your state VR agency through the Rehabilitation Services Administration state directory.

Section 6

Private Grants & Foundations

When insurance, Medicaid, and public programs do not fully cover an AAC device, private foundations and charitable organizations may be able to help. Below are established organizations that fund AAC and assistive technology.

United Healthcare Children's Foundation

Provides grants up to $5,000 for medical expenses not fully covered by insurance, including AAC devices. Open to families regardless of insurance provider.

uhccf.org

First Hand Foundation

Funds individual needs (including AT devices) for children whose families cannot afford them and have exhausted other options. No geographic restriction.

firsthandfoundation.org

Friends of Disabled Adults and Children (FODAC)

Provides assistive technology and medical equipment to individuals with disabilities. Accepts applications nationwide.

fodac.org

The Kelly Brush Foundation

Provides adaptive equipment grants for individuals with spinal cord injuries, including communication devices. Individual grants available.

kellybrushfoundation.org

Communication Devices Fund

Specifically focused on funding AAC and communication technology for individuals who cannot afford devices and have been denied by other funding sources.

Additional Resources

  • AACFundingHelp.com — resources and guides for navigating AAC funding
  • Local civic organizations (Lions Club, Rotary, Kiwanis) often fund AT for community members
  • GoFundMe and similar platforms can supplement other funding sources
  • Manufacturer financial assistance — some AAC device manufacturers offer payment plans or financial hardship programs
